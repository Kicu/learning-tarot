The Books Behind the Cards

Who supplied the meanings, who supplied the symbolism, and who taught us to read the pictures.

The three most important sources

For this RWS course, these explain the deck’s pictures, occult correspondences, and inherited card meanings.

  • The Pictorial Key to the Tarot

    Arthur Edward Waite · London, 1910/1911

    The companion to your RWS deck: Waite’s explanations of its symbols, his card meanings, and the layout now called the Celtic Cross. It records why he changed some traditional images, while preserving older fortune-telling meanings alongside Smith’s new scenes. This helps explain why a card’s printed meaning sometimes differs from what its picture suggests.

    English, 1922 edition ↗

    More about this book

    Written to accompany the Rider–Waite–Smith deck, the book explains Waite’s deliberate changes to older trump images. His divinatory sections also preserve inherited meanings, which can sit awkwardly beside Smith’s new scenes. The printed list and the picture therefore remain two pieces of evidence.

    Part III gives the ten-card method later known as the Celtic Cross, under Waite’s claim of an ancient Celtic method. The title does not establish that ancestry. The illustrated book followed the earlier, unillustrated Key to the Tarot. It was released in 1910 with a 1911 title-page date. Waite wrote the explanations; Smith created the card images.

  • Book T — The Tarot

    Golden Dawn · Britain, c. 1888–1896 · Associated with S. L. MacGregor Mathers

    The Golden Dawn’s system of suit elements, card titles, astrology, and Tree of Life correspondences, inherited in part by the RWS deck. It is the source of names such as “Lord of Unstable Effort” for the Seven of Swords, and associations such as Wands with Fire. Originally circulated as private teaching papers, it helps explain the deck’s occult symbolism, though it cannot account for every detail of Smith’s pictures.

    English source ↗

    More about this book

    Book T names the Seven of Swords “Lord of Unstable Effort” and assigns Wands to Fire. It coordinates several occult systems across the pack. The Fool takes Aleph and Air; Strength is linked to Leo and Justice to Libra. These relationships help explain choices inherited by the RWS deck.

    The papers circulated privately within the Golden Dawn around 1888–96, so there is no single original publication date. A public presentation of related material appeared as Liber LXXVIII in The Equinox I:8 (1912). Book T’s descriptions are not a complete script for Smith’s pictures, so its title cannot always tell you what the RWS scene will show.

    The related “Sub Spe” manuscript

    A Golden Dawn study text hand-copied by John Brodie-Innes under the motto Sub Spe. It explains how number and suit combine, supplying labels such as “Initiation,” “Opposition,” and “Accomplishment.” Mary K. Greer published it online in 2008; its original date is uncertain. Book T supplies the card titles; Sub Spe explains the number meanings.

    Book T and “Book of Thoth” are different names. The supposed Egyptian book belongs to Court de Gébelin’s origin myth. Crowley’s The Book of Thoth (1944) is a later explanation of the deck he developed with Frieda Harris, adapting Golden Dawn ideas to his own system. 1944 source ↗

  • The Tarot

    S. L. MacGregor Mathers · London, 1888

    An English guide that transmitted Etteilla-derived card meanings into occult tarot. Waite reused many of these lists, even when they differed from Smith’s pictures. This short public handbook combines upright and reversed meanings with instructions for fortune-telling and playing the game. Its practical word-lists distinguish it from the Golden Dawn’s Book T, with which it is easily confused.

    English text ↗

    More about this book

    The full title is The Tarot: Its Occult Signification, Use in Fortune-Telling, and Method of Play, Etc. Mathers openly identifies Etteilla as the main source of his divinatory meanings. The pamphlet supplies short upright and reversed lists, instructions for layouts, and rules for playing. It is a public introduction, published before the RWS deck existed.

    For the Seven of Swords, Mathers gives hope, confidence, desire, attempt, and wish. Waite repeats much of this list and adds quarrelling and a plan that may fail, even though Smith’s scene invites a reading about taking something away. This pamphlet and Book T are separate texts. Mathers is associated with both, and both can appear online under the title “The Tarot,” but this is the one to remember for inherited fortune-telling word-lists.

Other influential works

  • Le Monde primitif · Volume VIII

    Antoine Court de Gébelin · Paris, 1781

    The Egyptian origin story begins here: tarot is presented as ancient wisdom concealed in an ordinary pack of cards. A small section of this much larger work on ancient culture encouraged later occultists to search the cards for hidden religious teachings. Its lasting influence comes from that interpretation; the claimed Egyptian ancestry has no supporting historical evidence.

    French scan, volume VIII ↗

    More about this book

    Tarot appears in a small part of a much larger work about ancient culture. Court de Gébelin looked at the cards already being played in Europe and interpreted them as the remains of Egyptian religious knowledge. His claim encouraged later occultists to treat the pack as a symbolic text that could be decoded.

    Its lasting contribution is the Egyptian origin myth, which gave later occult writers a reason to search the cards for hidden teachings. No evidence establishes that Egyptian ancestry.

  • Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots

    Etteilla (Jean-Baptiste Alliette) · Paris / Amsterdam imprint, 1783–1785

    An early systematic tarot-reading manual, at the start of the fortune-telling tradition whose meanings later reach Mathers and Waite. Published in several parts, it turns the new Egyptian interpretation into a practical method, with upright and reversed meanings, dealing procedures, and card combinations. Etteilla and his followers helped establish the vocabulary that later readers inherited.

    French scan, second cahier ↗

    More about this book

    Published in several parts, Etteilla’s work develops a practical system of tarot divination within the new Egyptian framework. Upright and reversed meanings, procedures for dealing, and combinations of cards make reading into something a person can learn and repeat.

    His earlier Etteilla, ou manière de se récréer avec un jeu de cartes (1770) concerned ordinary playing cards; the tarot work began in 1783. Etteilla and his later school supplied many of the meanings subsequently transmitted through Mathers and Waite, so a meaning described as “Etteilla’s” may come from his followers rather than directly from this book.

  • Dogme et rituel de la haute magie

    Éliphas Lévi · Paris, 1854–1856

    The major link between tarot, Hebrew letters, and ceremonial magic, with imagery that later finds its way onto your cards. Known in English as Transcendental Magic, it connects the twenty-two trumps with the Hebrew alphabet within Lévi’s wider occult system. His Baphomet and Wheel of Fortune are especially useful for recognizing his influence on the RWS images.

    English translation, 1896 ↗

    More about this book

    Known in English as Transcendental Magic: Its Doctrine and Ritual, this work made tarot central to a wider system of magic. Lévi’s treatment connects the twenty-two trumps with the Hebrew alphabet and interprets the cards through his occult understanding of Kabbalah. Later writers borrowed and rearranged these correspondences: Lévi connects Aleph with the Magician, while the Golden Dawn assigns it to the Fool.

    The visual connection is especially memorable in the Devil. Waite discusses Lévi’s Baphomet while giving the RWS figure his own interpretation; he also acknowledges Lévi’s reconstruction of the Wheel of Fortune. Publication began in instalments in 1854, followed by the two-volume edition in 1855–56; Waite’s English translation appeared in 1896. The French and English titles therefore refer to the same underlying work.

  • A Complete Guide to the Tarot

    Eden Gray · New York, 1970

    A major popular guide in the RWS revival, combining inherited meanings with readings of the actions visible in Smith’s pictures. Gray helped make this approach accessible to a broad audience during the tarot revival of the 1960s and 1970s. Her influential Fool’s Journey treats the other trumps as experiences encountered by the Fool, giving readers a story through which to learn the sequence.

    Publisher · buy book or ebook ↗

    More about this book

    Gray brought RWS reading to a broad audience during tarot’s revival in the 1960s and 1970s. Her Seven of Swords illustrates the growing emphasis on the visible scene: the man’s removal of the swords supports a reading about taking what belongs to somebody else, alongside older meanings.

    Her Fool’s Journey presents the other trumps as experiences encountered by the Fool. It became an influential way of learning the sequence, but it is a modern interpretation rather than evidence of what the first card-makers intended. Her subsequent Mastering the Tarot (1971) also helped transmit variations of the Celtic Cross.

  • Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom

    Rachel Pollack · 1980 & 1983

    A detailed guide to interpreting the RWS images, with psychological and spiritual readings that go beyond memorizing a list of keywords. Pollack explores how a card’s composition, symbols, and relationships with other cards develop its meaning. Originally published in separate volumes on the Major and Minor Arcana, the work helped establish tarot as a practice of reflection and personal development.

    Publisher · preview & buy ↗

    More about this book

    Pollack explores how a card’s composition, symbolism, and relationship to other cards can support an interpretation. She treats Smith’s artwork as a source of meaning in its own right, including where a scene sits uneasily with Waite’s printed list.

    The first volume, on the Major Arcana, appeared in 1980; the second, on the Minor Arcana and readings, followed in 1983. Later editions brought them together. Pollack helped establish an approach to tarot centred on psychological insight, spiritual development, and sustained attention to the images.

The Game of Tarot: From Ferrara to Salt Lake City

Michael Dummett, with Sylvia Mann · 1980

A study of tarot’s history and games, grounded in documents and surviving packs. It established a scholarly account of the Italian game tradition and regional differences in trump order, helping separate the cards’ documented origins from later occult explanations. For this course, it helps explain how the trump sequence developed through regional forms of play, before occult writers gave it spiritual interpretations.
