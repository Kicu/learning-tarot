The Sixteen Courts

The court cards at a glance: the posture each rank sets, the sixteen cards in one grid, the family Book T hid inside the ranks, and the procedure for deciding who the person is. Compressed from lesson 6.

How to read a court card

StepLook atSay
1RankThe posture — Page learning it, Knight pursuing it, Queen keeping it, King running it.
2SuitThe business — will, feeling, mind or matter, as for any card (lesson 3).
3PictureThe temper: what this figure's face, seat or horse does to the posture. The picture wins any conflict, as always.
—Then decideWho is it? Another person, a side of yourself, or the approach being asked for — three questions below, and the choice made aloud.

Who is it? — the three questions, in order

AskQuestionIf yes
1Does this posture in this suit instantly name a real person around the question?Read it as that person.
2If not — is it a stance I am currently taking in the matter?Read it as that side of yourself.
3If neither — what would it mean to meet this question in this posture?Read it as the approach being asked for.
The deck never settles this for you — no rank, suit or picture detail marks a court card as person, self or approach. Saying the choice aloud, with the evidence pointed at, is part of an honest reading.

Invented · 1888

The family, the elements and the seniority are Golden Dawn doctrine from Book T, which also holds the tradition's default: "the Knights and Queens almost invariably represent actual men and women connected with the subject in hand." Waite printed the old game's rank names on the cards and this family's seniority in his instructions — the mismatch is his book's, not yours.

The Victorian castings — historical curiosity only

Waite's divination instructions cast people by colouring: Wands courts are "very fair people, with yellow or auburn hair, fair complexion and blue eyes"; Cups, "light brown or dull fair hair and grey or blue eyes"; Swords, "hazel or grey eyes, dark brown hair and dull complexion"; Pentacles, "very dark brown or black hair, dark eyes and sallow or swarthy complexions." His own escape clause follows immediately — "you can be guided on occasion by the known temperament of a person; one who is exceedingly dark may be very energetic" — and modern practice keeps only the temperament.
