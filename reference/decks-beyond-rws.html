The Decks Beyond the RWS

The course is built on one deck, the Rider–Waite–Smith of 1909. This sheet maps the rest of the landscape — the two other systems you will hear named, the RWS's own descendants, the modern decks people actually buy, and the Renaissance originals — with a note on how much of this course transfers to each. Researched and link-checked 2026-08-15.

Three systems, not hundreds of decks

Hundreds of decks are in print, and nearly all of them follow one of three patterns. The pattern matters more than the cover art, because it decides what the number cards carry — and so whether the picture-first method of lesson 5 has anything to work on.

RWSDecember 1909Full scenes on all forty number cards — Smith's innovation, after Sola Busca.Everything. This is the home deck.
Thothpainted 1938–43, published 1969Non-scenic pips with a one-word title printed on each card.Suits and arc — and lesson 4's titles nearly verbatim, since the printed words descend from Book T.

Tarot de Marseille is a pattern rather than a single authored deck: French and Swiss workshops reproduced it for centuries, from Jean Noblet's Paris deck of c. 1650 to Nicolas Conver's Marseille deck of 1760, which "became the standard deck in France" and is the ancestor of nearly every edition in print. Three current editions matter: Paul Marteau's Grimaud Ancien Tarot de Marseille (1930, reissued ever since), Yoav Ben-Dov's CBD Tarot restored directly from Conver, and the Camoin–Jodorowsky restoration (1997). Reading it is a different skill: with no scenes, everything rests on number, suit and the card's visual rhythm — the school Ben-Dov's free materials teach (overview).

The Thoth Tarot was conceived by Aleister Crowley and painted by Lady Frieda Harris over five years, 1938–1943; both died before it was published as a deck (commonly dated 1969, with a monochrome first printing recorded in 1968). It is the Golden Dawn's other descendant, and for this course the revealing one: the small cards print their titles on their faces, and those titles are Book T's, some revised by Crowley (card list, review). In course terms, Thoth prints the formula on the card; the RWS paints a scene that may overrule it.

Documented

The two cards this course knows best make the Thoth–Book T link checkable: the Seven of Swords, Book T's Unstable Effort, is printed Futility on the Thoth card, and the Eight of Cups, Book T's Abandoned Success, is printed Indolence (Thoth card table against Book T). Crowley kept the system and reworded some verdicts — there is no thief on his Seven of Swords, because there is no scene at all.

Documented

The family's headline number: "more than 100 million copies of Pamela Colman Smith's Tarot card designs … are in circulation in over 20 countries, making it the most popular set ever made" (Hyperallergic, 2019). That is a circulation estimate traced to the publisher, not an audited sales figure — quote it with that label.

The market behind the boom is documented too: "Tarot-deck sales in general are up 30 percent this year, after rising 30 percent in 2016 — the highest in 50 years," the New York Times News Service reported in 2017, quoting the RWS's own publisher.

Marketing, filtered

Publisher copy inflates, so two familiar claims were checked and dropped: Deviant Moon's "voted Top Tarot Deck of All Time" (the verified community vote puts it fourth), and Modern Witch's "over 400,000 copies sold" (publisher-only figure, no independent source found). Everything in the table above traces to a named, dated source.

The Renaissance originals, back in print

Both decks the course's history lessons lean on can be owned as facsimiles. The Visconti-Sforza (c. 1450, Milan) is the luxury hand-painted deck of the ducal court — unnumbered, untitled trumps and scene-less pips, made for play and prestige before divination existed; 74 cards survive, split between the Morgan Library in New York and two collections in Bergamo (overview). U.S. Games publishes a 74-card facsimile with the four lost cards recreated — the Devil and the Tower among them — and Lo Scarabeo sells a restored edition.

The Sola Busca (c. 1491) you already know from lesson 5 as the source Smith borrowed from. It is the oldest complete 78-card tarot in existence and the first with fully illustrated pips; Italy bought the original for €800,000 in 2009 for the Pinacoteca di Brera (overview), and Lo Scarabeo publishes the official Brera edition. Owning it means owning the proof that scenic minors are four centuries older than their meanings.

If you ever buy a second deck

Nothing in the mission requires one. When the moment comes, the choice is simpler than the variety suggests: any scene-faithful RWS redesign — Modern Witch, Ethereal Visions, anything in the family table — costs you nothing, because the reading you have learned moves over intact. A Marseille or a Thoth is a different instrument, and for that reason a good stress test: the Marseille forces you to read from lesson 4's stages alone, and the Thoth shows you Book T's verdicts printed on card stock, with no picture to overrule them. Either would sharpen what you know; neither is a good first home.
