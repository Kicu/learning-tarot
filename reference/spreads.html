Reading with Spreads

The table-side sheet for a live reading: the per-card procedure, the three-card frames, the rules that keep a reading honest, and Waite's ten Celtic Cross positions. Built by lesson 10; lesson 11 walks the Celtic Cross.

The procedure, per card

One procedure at reading time, no source arbitration (lesson 8). Meanings come from memory; the sheets are for checking afterwards.

StepDoNotes
1Say the memorized lineFrom the drills — the 56 sheet or the trumps sheet grades you afterwards, not during.
2Check the pictureThe detail that carries the meaning; the picture wins any conflict with a printed list.
3Number and suitNumber sets the stakes (which chapter of the story); suit sets the register (will, feeling, mind, matter).
4Special casesCourt card: the three ordered questions — person, self, or approach? The position often answers the first. Trump: raise the stakes of the whole question and say so in one extra sentence.
5Speak it into the positionThe position's half-sentence ("in the past…", "if nothing changes…"), completed by the card's meaning.

Three-card frames

FramePositions askReach for it when
Past · Present · FutureHow it came about · where it stands · where it heads if nothing changes — trajectory, never verdict.The default general question.
Problem · Cause · SolutionWhat is wrong · what feeds it · what would help.Something stuck; "why" matters most.
Option A · Option B · How to chooseEach path's character · what the choice turns on.A real decision, two live options.
You · Them · The relationshipYour side · their side · the thing between you.A question about two people.
Support · Advice · WarningCentre card answers; left supports it, right warns.Historic-style flavour — Tarot Heritage's adaptation of French and Italian authors.

The first four are today's consensus forms (Biddy Tarot Podcast, ep. 10); the last is from Tarot Heritage's Spreads page. The three-card spread has no dated origin — every published method from Etteilla (1783) through Mathers (1888) to Waite (1911) used ten cards or more.

Rules that keep a reading honest

RuleWhy
Frame before dealingDeclared afterwards, the frame flatters whatever appeared; declared first, it is a commitment the cards must meet.
One deal per questionRedrawing turns every reading into "deal until flattered". A new question earns a new deal.
Don't force the join"These two I can read; the third I would be inventing" is a legitimate — and impressive — sentence.
Future = trajectory"If nothing changes" is part of the position's meaning. No reading of this course predicts.
Reversed cards go uprightReversals are documented practice since Etteilla 1770, and parked as a post-course extra. Uprights-only is a recognized way to read.
Justify on demandEvery sentence traces to a memorized line plus a detail in the picture. If it doesn't, say so — and drill that card.

The Celtic Cross · Waite's ten positions

Printed in 1911 as "An Ancient Celtic Method of Divination" — his own preface admits "I do not think that it has been published" before, so the antiquity is Waite's label, not a documented lineage. Each position is a spoken formula; the quoted words are his (Pictorial Key, Part III §7). Lesson 11 walks the layout with the deck, including the Significator choice and Waite's two follow-up rules.

CardWaite's formulaIt asks
1"This covers him"The general influence, the atmosphere the matter sits in.
2"This crosses him"The nature of the obstacles.
3"This crowns him"The aim or ideal — the best not yet made actual.
4"This is beneath him"The foundation — what has already passed into actuality.
5"This is behind him"The influence just passing away.
6"This is before him"The influence coming into action in the near future.
7HimselfThe querent's own position or attitude in the circumstances.
8His houseThe environment — friends, position in life, tendencies at work.
9His hopes or fearsExactly that, and worth asking which of the two it is.
10What will comeThe culmination brought about by all the rest — read as trajectory here too.

Positions 1–6 form the cross around a chosen Significator; 7–10 stand in a line beside it. Waite's Significator rules (Knight = man of forty and upward, King = a man under forty) are on the courts sheet, with lesson 6's honest note on their age logic.
