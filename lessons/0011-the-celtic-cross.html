Lesson 11 · Tarot

The Celtic Cross

Waite's own ten-card method, from the book that came with your deck: a chosen Significator, ten spoken formulas, and the grammar of lesson 10 stretched to its full length.

The win

By the end of this page you have laid the full ten-card spread with your own deck — Significator chosen by Waite's rules, all ten formulas spoken from memory, the cross read as one scene and the staff as its commentary. This is the spread the mission names, learned in the one version that has a primary source.

Lesson 10 taught the whole idea already: a position is half a sentence, and the card you memorized completes it. The Celtic Cross adds no new theory — it is a longer sentence, with ten halves instead of three, and Waite wrote the halves out as words to be said at the table: "This covers him… This crosses him… This crowns him." He recommends the method "for obtaining an answer to a definite question" and closes by calling it "free from all complications" (Pictorial Key, Part III §7) — a large claim for ten cards, and this lesson's job is to make it feel true. What is genuinely new here is small: choosing a card to stand for the person asked about, learning the deal order cold, and reading ten cards in groups rather than one after another.

The most famous spread has the shortest paper trail

Documented

The spread first appears in print in the Pictorial Key (1911), as Part III §7, "An Ancient Celtic Method of Divination". Introducing it, Waite says only this about where it came from: "I offer in the first place a short process which has been used privately for many years past in England, Scotland and Ireland. I do not think that it has been published — certainly not in connexion with Tarot cards" (Part III §6). That sentence is the entire documented history: no earlier printing, manuscript or named source has ever been produced. Even the famous name is not his — the phrase "Celtic Cross" appears nowhere in the book (the full text can be searched); Waite's own words are that the cards "are now disposed in the form of a cross".

Invented · the "ancient" and the name

The "ancient Celtic" pedigree is Waite's label, resting on his word alone — the deck itself is Italian and the 1440s are its earliest record, so any genuinely ancient Celtic use is out of the question. The name "Celtic Cross" and today's many variants are the twentieth century's work. Eden Gray, whose handbooks drove the post-1960 revival (Wikipedia credits her with "an integral part in the creation of the contemporary interest in esoteric Tarot"), was still printing it as the "Keltic Cross" in Mastering the Tarot (1971), with the cards in a different order from Waite's (Benebell Wen's review) and fears split into a position of their own (Mary K. Greer). Biddy's current version begins at "the Present" and ends through an "Advice" card, and says plainly that "there are many different versions of the Celtic Cross, including differences in the order of the cards" (Biddy Tarot). This course reads Waite's version — it is the one with a primary source, and the source shipped with your deck.

First, choose who the reading is about

Before any card is dealt, Waite has the diviner pull one card to stand for the person or matter asked about — the Significator — and set it face up on the table. For a person, the choice runs through the court cards by the age table lesson 6 already puzzled through: a Knight "if the subject of inquiry is a man of forty years old and upward", a King "for any male who is under that age", a Queen "for a woman who is over forty years and a Page for any female of less age" — the enthroned monarch standing for the younger man, which makes sense only through Book T's family, where the horseman is the father (the courts sheet has the full table and the honest note). The suit comes from the person's temperament, and Waite's own escape clause applies: known character beats complexion. For a matter rather than a person, he allows any card whose meaning fits — his example is a question about a lawsuit, with Justice as the Significator, since "this has reference to legal affairs".

Two mechanical rules follow. The Significator leaves the pack — you shuffle and cut "the rest of the pack three times, keeping the faces of the cards downwards". And because two positions will hang on which way the Significator faces, Waite adds a note that should sound familiar from lesson 10's frame rule: if the card "cannot be said to face either way, the Diviner must decide before beginning the operation which side he will take it as facing". Declared before dealing, like everything else worth trusting.

The cross and the staff

The first six cards build the cross, and each lands with its formula spoken aloud. Card 1 covers the Significator: "the influence which is affecting the person or matter of inquiry generally, the atmosphere of it in which the other currents work." Card 2 lies sideways across it — "This crosses him" — for "the nature of the obstacles in the matter". Card 3 crowns, above: the aim or ideal, "the best that can be achieved under the circumstances, but that which has not yet been made actual". Card 4 sits beneath: "the foundation or basis of the matter, that which has already passed into actuality". Cards 5 and 6 take the sides — behind him the influence "that is just passed, or is now passing away", before him the one "that is coming into action and will operate in the near future" — behind at the Significator's back, before on the side he faces (all quotes: Pictorial Key, Part III §7).

The last four are dealt in a vertical line to the right of the cross, bottom to top — the staff. Seven is "himself… its position or attitude in the circumstances"; eight "his house, that is, his environment… his position in life, the influence of immediate friends"; nine "his hopes or fears in the matter" — and it is worth saying aloud which of the two you are looking at; ten is "what will come, the final result, the culmination which is brought about by the influences shewn by the other cards". Waite tells the diviner to concentrate hardest on this last card — and the course adds its standing rule on top: in a reading that refuses to predict, positions 6 and 10 are trajectories, where things head if nothing changes.

Ten cards is not ten sentences

Read one after another, ten cards produce a list, and a list is not a reading. The workable approach is to read the cross as one scene first — 1 and 2 are the heart of it, situation and obstacle; 3 over 4 is a vertical axis, the ideal over the ground it would have to grow from; 5 and 6 are time flowing through the middle, what recedes and what arrives. Then the staff comments on that scene from four distances: the querent inside it (7), the people and circumstances around it (8), the feelings underneath it (9), and where the whole thing points (10). Today's teachers give the same advice in their own words — Biddy: "you'll need to master the dynamics between the cards" — and the pairs worth checking against each other are 3 and 10 (does the outcome resemble the aim?) and 4 and 9 (does the fear match the ground, or outrun it?).

Waite closes the chapter with two follow-up rules of his own. If the tenth card "is of a dubious nature, from which no final decision can be drawn", he allows the operation to be repeated once, with that tenth card as the new Significator; and if the tenth card is a court card, the matter "falls ultimately into the hands of a person represented by that card" — a fresh operation with that person as Significator shows what their influence will be. Both sit close to the line lesson 10 drew against redrawing, so the course keeps them on a leash: a repeat is a new operation announced aloud, used at most once, and only when the tenth card genuinely cannot conclude — never because the conclusion displeases.

A worked reading

A friend — thirty-six — has been offered the lead of another team and cannot decide whether to take it. The matter is a venture, so the Significator is the King of Wands: a man under forty, by Waite's odd but explicit rule, in the suit of will and enterprise. His facing is declared aloud before the shuffle: left. The ten cards land, and the memorized lines from the master sheet go into their positions:

Grouped, the cross is one scene: a trained man in a noisy contest, aiming at open-sky work from a base of recognized craft, the old post already fading behind him and the planning of the move arriving in front. The staff comments: he is charging at it, there is one honest adviser in his house he has not yet asked, the fear is a night-time size rather than a daytime one, and the road ends on solid footing if the course holds. The pairs earn their keep here — the Sun at 3 and the Ace of Pentacles at 10 agree with each other, and the Nine of Swords at 9 is flatly contradicted by the Three of Pentacles at 4: the dread outruns the ground. None of that tells him to take the job; it tells him he has already left the old one, and gives him a Queen of Swords to talk to before he signs.

Do this now · with your deck

Twenty minutes, the full spread, on a question of your own.

  1. Choose the Significator aloud. Yourself, another person by the age-and-temperament table, or a matter card in Waite's lawsuit style. Say why, declare which way the card faces, and set it down.
  2. Say the question, then shuffle. Waite's mechanics exactly: the rest of the pack, cut three times, faces down. Any card that lands reversed turns upright, as always.
  3. Deal the cross speaking the six formulas from memory. Then the staff, seven to ten. If a formula will not come, finish the deal, then run the ordering drill below until it does.
  4. Read the cross as one scene, then the staff as commentary. Each card gets the usual procedure — memorized line, picture, number and suit, courts and trumps by their rules — spoken into its position. Then check the pairs: 3 against 10, 4 against 9.
  5. Justify on demand, and book the rematch. Point at one detail per claim, name any card you would be inventing, and lay the spread again in two days on a fresh question. When it runs smoothly twice, deal it for a friend — that reading is the mission's finish line.

    Check yourself

    One attempt per question, no scrolling back up.

    The querent is a man of thirty-five asking about himself. Waite's Significator table hands him…

    • A King — the Knight is reserved for men of forty and upward
    • A Knight — the King is reserved for men of forty and upward
    • A Page — the court ranks stand for suits rather than for people

    Waite's rule runs against intuition: a Knight for "a man of forty years old and upward", a King "for any male who is under that age" — the enthroned monarch is the younger man. Lesson 6 traced the reason to Book T's court family, where the horseman is the father.

    The oldest thing anyone can actually document about the Celtic Cross is…

    • Waite printing it in 1911 while calling it privately used before
    • Etteilla printing it in 1783 among his first tarot card methods
    • Druids laying it out in Ireland centuries before tarot cards existed

    The Pictorial Key is the earliest record anyone has produced, and Waite himself claims only private use "in England, Scotland and Ireland", adding "I do not think that it has been published". Etteilla's printed methods were his own large layouts, and the deck is Italian, first recorded in the 1440s — no ancient Celt ever saw one.

    Blind, from the formulas: the fourth card, "This is beneath him", shows…

    • The foundation — what has already passed into actuality
    • The obstacle — what has already crossed into opposition
    • The ideal — what has not yet been achieved

    Waite's own gloss: "the foundation or basis of the matter, that which has already passed into actuality". The obstacle is card 2, lying across the Significator, and the not-yet-actual ideal is card 3, the crown — beneath and behind are the two prepositions worth keeping carefully apart.

    The tenth card of your spread turns out to be the Queen of Swords. Waite's own instruction is…

    • The matter ends in that person's hands — read her as Significator next
    • The deal is void when courts conclude it — shuffle and lay again
    • The court card outranks the frame — switch to a smaller three-card spread

    His words: the subject "falls ultimately into the hands of a person represented by that card", and he suggests a fresh operation with that court as the new Significator. The course allows it — announced aloud, as a new operation — because it digs into the answer rather than rejecting it.

    Blind, from the drill: the Seven of Swords lands second — "This crosses him". Say the card's line, then pick the obstacle.

    • Something being quietly lifted away — stealth at work against the matter
    • Something being openly fought over — rivals contesting claims to the matter
    • Something being slowly carried over — the worry travelling with the matter

    The sheet's line for the tiptoeing man with five swords is deception — "acting alone, getting away with something, or trying to" — and spoken into position 2 that stealth becomes the obstacle itself. The open fight is the Five of Wands; the worry carried across the water is the Six of Swords.
