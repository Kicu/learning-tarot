Lesson 08 · Tarot

The Suit Cards by Heart

The systems of lessons 4 to 6 explain the fifty-six suit cards; they do not recite them for you at the table. This lesson turns the explanations into memory — one sheet, one drill, repeated on a schedule.

The win

By the end of this page you have run the first round of a drill you will repeat over the next two weeks. After those rounds, a drawn suit card names its meaning before you have time to reason about it — and lessons 4, 5 and 6 are left doing the job they were always for: explaining why the meaning is what it is.

Where the mental arithmetic belongs

You made an observation after lesson 6 that deserves a straight answer: every rule this course has taught arrives with exceptions, and picking a card's meaning now takes real arithmetic — is this one straight from the formula, is it the picture, is it a court card. The arithmetic is real, and it is not a sign you have misunderstood anything. The Rider–Waite–Smith meanings were compiled, not computed: roughly half of Waite's printed lists copy the Etteilla school, Book T's formula ignores several of Smith's scenes, and a few scenes were borrowed from a 1491 deck — the ancestry sheet holds the tally. Nobody derives these meanings from first principles at the table, because no principle generates them.

The fix is to move the arithmetic to the moment where it belongs. Learning a card is one moment, and there the systems earn their keep: knowing that five is the opposition stage and Cups the suit of feeling makes the Five of Cups nearly impossible to forget once you have seen the mourner and the two cups still standing behind him — and your mission asks you to justify readings, not merely deliver them. Reading a card is the other moment, and it involves no sources at all. You recognise the card and say its meaning; if memory fails, you fall back on the standing procedure — say the scene in one sentence, let the number set the stakes and the suit the register. Practised readers work exactly this way. The meanings are known by heart, and the systems sit underneath as the reason those meanings are what they are.

So your instinct to memorise is correct, with one correction. The single thing to commit to cannot be one source, because whichever you picked would contradict the deck in your hands somewhere — Waite's own book keeps Etteilla's "hope, confidence" in the Seven of Swords list, printed under the thief Smith drew. The single thing is one sheet: a row per card that settles what you say, with the title column left in as the memory glue.

The sheet

The Fifty-Six Suit Cards is that sheet, built for this drill. Each row gives the Book T title or court posture (the glue), one literal sentence about Smith's scene (what you can point at), and the meaning as this course says it aloud — tuned to the picture and checked against today's mainstream. Print it and keep it with the deck; the drill below grades you against its rows.

First round, with the real deck

  1. Separate the fifty-six suit cards and set the trumps aside. Pick one suit — start with whichever felt least familiar in lesson 3 — and shuffle its fourteen cards face down.
  2. Turn the top card and, before anything else, say two things aloud: one literal sentence about the scene, then the meaning in one line.
  3. Check the card's row on the sheet. If your line carried the row's point, the card goes to a knew it pile; if not, read the full row aloud once and put the card on a missed it pile.
  4. When the suit runs out, reshuffle the missed pile and repeat it until that pile is empty.
  5. Stop there. One suit per sitting is the full dose — the next suit belongs to the next sitting, and the gap between sittings is part of the method.

Second round, without the picture

The physical drill hands you the scene for free, and at the table the card in front of you always will. The drill below is deliberately harder: it gives you only the name, and the scene itself becomes part of what you retrieve. Say the scene and the meaning aloud, reveal, and grade yourself honestly — a missed card comes back a few cards later in the same run.

The schedule

A drill run once teaches you very little; the spacing is where the memory is built. Run today's suit again in two days, add the next suit in the same sitting, and keep that rhythm until all four suits have had three passes — then drop to one mixed "All" run a week. What feels smooth within a sitting is not yet stored; the stumble two days from now, corrected on the spot, is what stores it. Expect the drill to feel harder on day three than it did today. That is the method working, not you failing.

Keep grading yourself against the row's point rather than its exact words. "Regret that ignores the two cups still standing" and "mourning the spilled cups while something remains" are the same answer; a meaning recited perfectly for the wrong card is not.

Read this alongside the drill

Primary source · a chapter at a time

Rachel Pollack, Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom (1980, revised 1997) — the flagship of reading Smith's scenes seriously, one card at a time, and honest about the seams ("the picture almost contradicts the meaning," she says of the Two of Swords). No free full text exists; the book is worth owning. A sane free alternative is Joan Bunning's Learn Tarot course, card pages included. Both are interpretation, not history — the dated record stays in the ancestry sheet.
