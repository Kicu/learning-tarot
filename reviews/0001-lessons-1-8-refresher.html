Review 01 · Tarot

A Refresher on the First Eight Lessons

Thirteen mixed questions and a short deck drill covering everything from lessons 1 to 8 — history, suits, numbers, courts, and the first eleven trumps. A few days away from the cards is not lost ground: a gap followed by honest retrieval is precisely what moves the rows into long-term memory.

The win

By the end of this page you know which parts of the first eight lessons are still solid and which rows need a rerun before lesson 9 — and every missed question names the exact section to reread.

The questions are deliberately mixed rather than grouped by lesson, because switching between topics forces a fuller search of memory than answering five in a row about the same page. Take them cold, with the reference sheets closed, and treat every miss as useful news about where a reread will do the most work. The wrong options are real lines from neighbouring cards and rules, so read all three before choosing.

Check yourself

One attempt per question, and the sheets stay closed until the end.

From lesson 1: the documented timeline of the deck in your hands runs…

  • Italian card game, then French occult rereading, then the 1909 redesign
  • Egyptian temple wisdom, then Italian card game, then the 1909 redesign
  • French occult teaching, then Italian card game, then the 1909 redesign

The paper trail starts with the trionfi game in 1440s Italy, the occult rereading begins with de Gébelin in 1781 France, and Waite and Smith redesigned the deck in 1909. The Egyptian temple story is the 1781 invention itself — lesson 1's whole point is that it postdates the game by three centuries.

In the parade of trumps, Strength, the Hermit, the Wheel of Fortune and Justice come directly after the Chariot because they show…

  • the forces no victory protects you from — character, time, luck, law
  • the four cardinal virtues in the order the Church taught them
  • the four stations of society ranked from the lowest to highest

Lesson 8's last stretch holds Fortitude, Time, Fortune and Justice — the things that still outrank a fresh victor. Only two of the four are cardinal virtues, and the stations of society are the parade's first stretch, trumps I to V.

Blind from lesson 7: the Five of Cups. The scene and the line to say are…

  • Mourning the spilled cups while two still stand — loss grieved so completely it overlooks what remains
  • Walking away from the stacked cups under the moon — leaving something whole because it stopped satisfying
  • Sitting with arms crossed ignoring the fourth cup — apathy and discontent with what is already there

The Five of Cups — Book T's Loss in Pleasure — mourns three spilled cups while two stand untouched and a bridge leads home. Walking away from the stack is the Eight of Cups, and the ignored fourth cup is the Four; both sit close by on the master sheet, which is why the drill keeps mixing them up.

Nine or ten? Lesson 4's picture test says the nine shows one figure alone, still able to act. The ten shows…

  • the result landed in a wider world
  • the same figure finally able to rest
  • the stage's conflict at its sharpest point

Book T's own contrast: the nine holds "executive power" on a firm basis, while the ten is "the matter thoroughly and definitely determined" — the outcome has landed among other people, like the family under the archway or the town ahead of the man carrying ten staves. The sharpest conflict belongs to the fives.

The court ladder from lesson 6 runs Page, Knight, Queen, King. As postures toward the suit's business, that is…

  • learning it, pursuing it, keeping it, running it
  • pursuing it, learning it, running it, keeping it
  • learning it, keeping it, pursuing it, running it

The Page is learning the suit's business, the Knight is pursuing it, the Queen is keeping it, and the King is running it. The ladder is modern consensus rather than 1909 doctrine, but it holds across all four suits and is the fastest way into any court card.

Blind from lesson 8: the High Priestess lands in a spread. The line to say is…

  • What is not yet being told — secrets kept, the answer still behind the veil
  • Fruitfulness and nurture — abundance, the things that grow only because they are being tended
  • Fairness and consequence — the deserving side, a decision that binds when it is made

Every detail Smith painted is holding something back — the half-covered scroll, the sealed veil, the crescent at her feet — and for once Waite's printed list matches the picture exactly: "secrets, mystery, the future as yet unrevealed… silence." The fruitfulness line belongs to the Empress, and the decision that binds to Justice.

Take your own job, as lesson 3 did: when the boredom and the stalled ambition are what bother you, and when it is really the salary, the suits are…

  • Wands for the ambition, Pentacles for the salary
  • Pentacles for the ambition, Wands for the salary
  • Swords for the ambition, Pentacles for the salary

Suit names the kind of problem, never the topic, so the same job moves between suits as the question changes. Stalled ambition is a question about will and appetite, which is Wands; the salary is a question about material terms, which is Pentacles.

A Marseille deck numbers Justice VIII and Strength XI; yours reverses them. The deck that is "wrong" is…

  • neither deck — the swap is doctrine, not a correction
  • the Marseille — Waite corrected a longstanding error in numbering
  • the RWS — a printer's error that Waite never acknowledged

Book T runs the trumps against the zodiac in sign order, which only works if the lion card holds the Leo slot at eight and the scales the Libra slot at eleven. Waite printed that numbering and declined to explain it — "for reasons which satisfy myself" — so a deck numbered the old way is declining his edit, not making an error.

A trump lands in an otherwise minor-card spread. Structure alone tells you…

  • the question itself is larger than one afternoon's business
  • the surrounding cards must all be read as trumps
  • the trump names a person standing near the question

Lesson 2 filed the trumps as the deck's big register: when one appears, the question is about the shape of things rather than one afternoon of them. Naming a person is what court cards do, and a trump changes nothing about how its neighbours are read.

Waite prints "treason, dissimulation, roguery" for the Hermit, yet the course reads the card as the solitary seeker. The rule deciding that is…

  • the picture wins any conflict with a printed list
  • the number wins any conflict with a printed list
  • the oldest list wins any conflict with newer lists

The rule from lesson 5, extended to the trumps in lesson 8: stage from the number, plot from the picture, and the picture wins any conflict with a printed list. Waite's Hermit entry carries the older fortune-teller's phrases, while the 1909 picture shows a seeker lighting the way — and modern practice follows the picture.

Blind from lesson 7: the Knight of Pentacles. In Smith's picture, his horse is…

  • standing still — the pursuit that plods and delivers
  • at full gallop — the charge that scatters enemies
  • rearing upward — the departure that cannot wait longer

Lesson 6 read the four knights by how their horses move, and his is the only one standing still, looking out with its rider over a ploughed field — slow useful work, routine that delivers. The full gallop belongs to the Knight of Swords, and the rearing horse to the Knight of Wands.

The Chariot's drill line pairs victory with a warning, because Waite's own upright list includes…

  • presumption
  • abandonment
  • corruption

Waite's upright list runs "succour, providence; also war, triumph, presumption, vengeance, trouble." The card shows the win at the moment of celebration, and celebration is exactly when overreach begins. Corruption sits in the Hermit's printed list, and abandonment belongs to a card the course has not reached yet.

The stage words for the fives and the sixes, in the order's own vocabulary, are…

  • Opposition, then Accomplishment
  • Accomplishment, then Opposition
  • Opposition, then Realization

Five is Opposition — the strife row, where every suit hits friction — and six is Accomplishment, the recovery after it. Realization is the four, the stage where the structure first holds. The number sets the stakes; the words are Sub Spe's, from lesson 4's table.

Do this now · with your deck

Ten minutes, the whole deck.

  1. The parade, blind. Pull trumps I to XI, shuffle them, and turn them one at a time. For each, say the 1450 identity and the table line aloud before checking the trumps sheet. Anything missed goes to the side for a second pass at the end.
  2. Three suit cards, cold. Shuffle the fifty-six suit cards and draw three. For each, say the title, the scene and the table line, then check the master sheet. A missed card is one to bring back in two days, not a verdict.
  3. One court, three questions. Draw until a court card appears and run lesson 6's procedure aloud — a person around the question, a side of yourself, or the approach being asked for — and say which of the three you chose.

If more than a couple of suit cards missed, run one full suit in lesson 7's drill before moving on. When the rows feel solid again, lesson 9 is already written and waiting: the last ten trumps and the Fool, which completes the sequence and opens the first drill on all twenty-two.
